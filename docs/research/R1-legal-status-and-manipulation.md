# R1 — Legal status, manipulation rules and market-structure risks for a single-account automated intraday system

Research date: 2026-10-16. Scope: one individual, own money only, Alpaca margin account (leverage disabled by configuration), long-only, listed US stocks $1–$5, minutes-to-hours holds, flat by close, software places limit orders automatically, AI coding agent helps build/review but never trades.

Where only secondary sources were reachable, or where excerpts were cut off, this is marked **UNCONFIRMED**. Nothing here is legal advice.

## 1. Registration: none needed for own-account trading; what would change that

**Short answer.** Trading your own money with your own software requires no SEC, FINRA or state registration or license. The three doors that would change that are (a) advising or managing anyone else's money for compensation (Advisers Act, state adviser laws), (b) publishing or selling signals (same statute; the publisher exclusion is narrow), and (c) pooling money (Investment Company Act + Securities Act/Reg D + adviser status). Also now moot for this design: the FINRA pattern-day-trader $25,000 rule was eliminated effective June 4, 2026.

**Detail.**

*Why own-account trading is unregulated.*
- Exchange Act §3(a)(5) defines "dealer" but excludes "a person that buys or sells securities ... for such person's own account, either individually or in a fiduciary capacity, but not as a part of a regular business." The SEC's broker-dealer guide calls this the trader/dealer distinction: a trader profits from price movement on its own strategy; a dealer quotes two-sided markets as a business. Individuals trading for themselves are treated as traders. The SEC's 2024 rules that would have widened "dealer" (Rules 3a5-4 and 3a44-2, with a $50M total-assets exemption) were vacated by the N.D. Texas on Nov 21, 2024 and the SEC dropped its appeal in Feb 2025, so that threat is gone.
- Advisers Act §202(a)(11) defines an investment adviser as a person who, **for compensation**, is **in the business** of **advising others** about securities (or issuing reports/analyses). All three elements must be present (SEC Release IA-1092, 1987). Trading one's own account advises no one; the definition is not triggered.
- Broker: a "broker" effects transactions "for the account of others" (§3(a)(4)). Buying or selling for another person, even a relative, is brokering and is not covered by the trader exception.
- Market Access Rule (Exchange Act Rule 15c3-5) obligations (pre-trade price/size/credit controls, erroneous-order checks) sit on the broker-dealer whose MPID is used (Alpaca), not on the API customer. You will feel these as Alpaca-side rejections and limits, not as your own compliance duties.

*Door (a): managing or advising other people's money.*
- Compensation is read broadly: any economic benefit (fee, profit share, "I'll cover your dinner") can satisfy it; IA-1092 says advising need not be your principal business, only an activity with some regularity, and "holding yourself out" as an adviser suffices (United States v. Miller, 3d Cir.).
- Federal vs state: Advisers Act §203A. Advisers with less than $100M regulatory AUM generally register with their **state** (Rule 203A-1 buffer: must register with SEC at $110M; may stay SEC-registered down to $90M). Advisers to private funds only with <$150M private-fund AUM are "exempt reporting advisers" (Rule 203(m)-1) that still file Form ADV Part 1 with the SEC and may still have state obligations.
- State de minimis: Advisers Act §222(d) bars a state from requiring registration of an adviser with no place of business in the state that had **fewer than six** clients resident there in the preceding 12 months. But that protects you only in states where you have no place of business. In your **home** state (where you sit), most states require registration from client #1 unless a state-specific exemption applies; some states (e.g., CT, WI) phrase it as "five or fewer" clients and apply it only to out-of-state advisers. The old federal "fewer than 15 clients" private adviser exemption (§203(b)(3)) was repealed by Dodd-Frank in 2011; do not rely on it.
- Family: the "family office" exclusion (Rule 202(a)(11)(G)-1, adopted 2011, amended 2016) requires (i) no clients other than "family clients" (family members, certain key employees, family trusts/estates/charities/wholly-owned entities), (ii) wholly owned and controlled by family members/entities, and (iii) no holding out to the public as an adviser. **Friends are not family clients**; a single non-family client, outside owner or public marketing destroys the exclusion. Note the rule is written for a *company*; an individual informally trading a sibling's account is not a "family office" in the rule's sense and would need to analyze adviser status directly. Managing a spouse's or your own joint/IRA accounts is generally treated as managing your own household money, but the moment there is compensation from someone else, the analysis changes. **UNCONFIRMED** detail: the involuntary-transfer cure period (proposal said 4 months; one summary says 1 year).

*Door (b): publishing or selling signals.*
- §202(a)(11)(D) excludes "the publisher of any bona fide newspaper, news magazine or business or financial publication of general and regular circulation." Lowe v. SEC, 472 U.S. 181 (1985), held that impersonal, disinterested, regularly scheduled publications to the general public fall within the exclusion; the Court contrasted a genuine publication with a "tout" (promotional material). Elements that push toward adviser status: personalized or account-specific advice, advice tied to the publisher's own trading or positions, irregular "alerts" sent when the publisher decides, interactive one-to-one communication. A paid signal feed emitting "buy XYZ now at 2.31" alerts that you yourself trade is not disinterested commentary; the SEC sanctioned Weiss Research (2006) as an unregistered adviser for an auto-trading newsletter where subscribers' brokers executed the newsletter's trades. Treat "selling signals" as presumptively adviser activity requiring registration (state or SEC) unless counsel says otherwise.
- Separate problem: if you ever publish signals while holding the position, the anti-fraud rules (scalping, Rule 10b-5; SEC v. Capital Gains, 1963) apply regardless of registration.

*Door (c): pooling money.*
- A pooled vehicle is a "security" (an investment contract) whose offer must be registered or exempt. Reg D Rule 506(b): unlimited accredited investors, up to 35 non-accredited (who must be sophisticated and receive registered-offering-like disclosure); no general solicitation; Form D within 15 days of first sale plus state notice filings.
- The pool is an "investment company" unless excluded: §3(c)(1) (not more than 100 beneficial owners, no public offering) or §3(c)(7) (qualified purchasers only).
- You, as manager, become an adviser to a private fund (see state/ERA thresholds above). Many states follow the NASAA model rule exempting private-fund advisers only where the 3(c)(1) fund's investors are all "qualified clients" (Rule 205-3: $1.1M under management or $2.2M net worth) if you charge performance fees.
- Investment clubs: the SEC's investor bulletin says a club in which **all** members actively participate generally is not an investment company and its interests often need not be registered, but if **one** member is paid to select investments, that member may be an investment adviser.

*Pattern day trading / margin.*
- FINRA Rule 4210's pattern-day-trader provisions ($25,000 minimum equity, 4-in-5-days test) were replaced by a risk-based intraday margin standard: SEC approved Apr 14, 2026 (Rel. 34-105226), FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10, effective June 4, 2026, with a firm transition period to Oct 20, 2027. The $2,000 minimum equity for a margin account remains. Brokers may implement at different paces; confirm Alpaca's current treatment. (Sources are mostly broker blogs; the Federal Register notice of filing and ACA/Wilson Sonsini alerts are the firmest.)

**Design implications.**
- No registration gate for the system as scoped. Document in the repo README that the system is single-user, own-account, no third-party money, no distribution of signals, so a future contributor does not casually add a "share my picks" feature.
- Build no multi-account or sub-account abstraction. If a second Alpaca account key ever appears in config, that is a legal design decision, not an engineering one.
- Keep signal outputs private (no public dashboard, Discord bot, or paid feed). If you later want to publish, that is a registration question first.
- Even with the PDT rule gone, keep the account equity comfortably above $2,000 and expect Alpaca to apply intraday margin monitoring; an equity-based kill switch in the system is cheap insurance.

**Confidence:** High on the core (own-account trading unregulated; adviser definition elements; §222(d); family-office conditions; Reg D/3(c)(1) numbers; PDT repeal). Medium on state-by-state home-state thresholds and family-office edge cases.

**Consult a professional?** Not for the current scope. **Yes, before** managing any other person's money (including friends or extended family), selling or publicly posting signals, or forming any pooled vehicle.

## 2. Market manipulation rules that bind any trader, and what recent cases charged

**Short answer.** In equities there is no spoofing-specific statute (Dodd-Frank's anti-spoofing provision, CEA §4c(a)(5)(C), covers only commodities/futures). Spoofing and layering in stocks are charged under Exchange Act §9(a)(2) (series of transactions creating actual or apparent active trading or raising/depressing price to induce others) and §10(b)/Rule 10b-5(a),(c) (schemes to defraud), often with Securities Act §17(a)(1),(3). Wash sales and matched orders are §9(a)(1). Marking the close is charged under §9(a)(2)/10(b). Every one of these applies to "any person", not just broker-dealers. FINRA Rules 2020 and 5210.03 bind member firms, which is why **brokers surveil and close retail accounts** for these patterns; the broker warning-then-closure sequence appears in three of the four 2025–2026 individual cases below. The common thread in all charged conduct: **orders placed with intent to cancel, on the opposite side from the trader's real intent, to move price, in thin books.** A long-only system that only ever places orders it wants filled, repriced toward the market, is structurally far from that fact pattern; the residual risks are order-to-trade ratios, closing-minute activity, and inadvertent self-matching.

**Detail.**

*Statutory map.*
- §9(a)(1): wash sales (no change in beneficial ownership) and matched orders (entering an order knowing a substantially matching opposite order has been or will be entered by the same or affiliated person) **for the purpose of** creating a false or misleading appearance of active trading or of the market. Intent element; incidental self-matches without purpose are not violations (FINRA Rule 5210.02 treats unintentional self-trades from unrelated strategies as bona fide, but requires firms to prevent patterns of them).
- §9(a)(2): "a series of transactions ... creating actual or apparent active trading ... or raising or depressing the price ... for the purpose of inducing the purchase or sale of such security by others." Used in every 2025–2026 spoofing complaint below.
- §10(b)/Rule 10b-5(a),(c): scheme liability; the SEC has used it for spoofing since at least SEC v. Lek Securities/Avalon (S.D.N.Y. 2017 motion-to-dismiss denial; 2019 jury verdict against Lek), where "layering" was defined as placing multiple non-bona fide limit orders on one side at different prices to shift apparent supply/demand, executing on the other side, then cancelling the layers.
- Dodd-Frank §747 / CEA §4c(a)(5)(C): "bidding or offering with the intent to cancel the bid or offer before execution" — commodities only; criminal (United States v. Coscia, 7th Cir. 2017). Equities spoofing is prosecuted criminally as wire fraud/securities fraud (18 U.S.C. §1348) when DOJ joins (e.g., DOJ's parallel case against Mingran Wang, 2026).
- FINRA Rule 5210.03 (effective Dec 15, 2016; Reg. Notice 17-22) prohibits members from engaging in or **facilitating** two patterns: (1) layering as defined above; (2) placing an order inside the NBBO and then an opposite-side order to execute against participants who joined the new inside market. FINRA may issue expedited cease-and-desist orders. FINRA also sends "cross-market report cards" to firms whose customers show potential layering/spoofing. This is the machinery that produces broker warnings and account closures for retail customers.
- FINRA Reg. Notice 15-09 (2015) describes effective supervision for algorithmic trading: risk assessment, code development controls, testing/validation, trading-system controls (including kill switches and message-rate limits), compliance. It addresses member firms, but it is a free template for your own controls.

*2023–2026 enforcement against individuals (equities/options; retail-style accounts).*
1. **SEC v. Ryan N. Cole** (E.D. Cal., LR-26371, Aug 11, 2025). Trader at a financial firm, trading thinly traded **options**: placed spoof orders across neighboring options series to move prices, executed genuine multi-leg IOC orders at the moved prices, cancelled spoofs; lied to his firm's compliance inquiry and was fired. Charged §17(a)(1),(3); §10(b)/10b-5(a),(c); §9(a)(2). Settled: disgorgement $234,803 + interest + $70,441 penalty, five-year trading restriction reported.
2. **SEC v. Artur Khachatryan** (C.D. Cal., LR-26445, filed Dec 16, 2025). Individual trading his own and friends'/relatives' accounts, roughly two years. Placed non-bona-fide orders on one side **outside regular hours when books were thin**, executed on the opposite side at the moved price, cancelled the spoofs, then repeated on the other side to lock in. After brokers restricted/closed his accounts he opened accounts in other people's names and misrepresented who would trade them. Charged incl. §9(a)(2). Settled: disgorgement $373,885, interest $22,629, penalty $112,165.
3. **SEC v. Mingran Wang** (N.D. Cal., LR-26574, filed June 24, 2026; parallel DOJ guilty plea, 4:26-cr-00288). Spoofed >150 thinly traded **ADRs**, Oct 2021–Nov 2024, thousands of spoof orders, >$1.3M gains. Placed non-bona-fide orders at one brokerage and executed real trades at a **different** brokerage (to defeat single-broker surveillance). Told investigators he tried "to walk the price up [to] my advantage." Charged §17(a)(1),(3); §10(b)/10b-5; §9(a)(2).
4. **SEC v. Frank Cerisano Jr.** (D. Nev., LR-26606, filed Aug 10, 2026; final judgment Sept 17, 2026). Las Vegas individual, May 2021–Apr 2025, ~$1.1M gains, **extended-hours** spoofing in thin books; a broker warned him then closed his account; he moved to other brokers. Settled: ~$1.12M disgorgement, ~$334,700 penalty, five-year trading restriction.
5. Also noted: SEC FY2025 enforcement results cite a California individual spoofing case with ~$234k gains (this is Cole). I did not find a 2024–2026 DOJ case against a purely retail equities trader other than the Wang parallel; older DOJ equity cases are Sarao (futures) and hacking-based matched-order schemes (Willner, 2017). FINRA's monthly disciplinary reports for 2025–2026 show firm-level sanctions for layering/spoofing surveillance gaps (e.g., alerts that excluded orders joining or improving the NBBO), confirming that broker surveillance of exactly these patterns is an active exam priority.

*What the cases teach about "intent" evidence.* Courts and regulators infer intent to cancel from statistics relative to peers: in Coscia, 96% of a market's cancellations were his; his large orders filled 0.08% vs small orders 35.6%; order-to-trade ratio 1,600% vs peers 90–260%; large orders algorithmically pulled whenever they risked filling. The CFTC's 2013 interpretive guidance (78 FR 31890), which the SEC's equity cases track in substance, says: orders, modifications and cancellations submitted "as part of a legitimate, good-faith attempt to consummate a trade" are not spoofing; good-faith cancellation of partially filled orders is fine; properly placed stop-loss orders are fine; recklessness is not enough; but a single episode can be a violation. Four illustrative patterns of spoofing: overloading the quotation system, delaying others' executions, creating false depth via multiple bids/offers, creating artificial price movements.

**Design implications (concrete rules).**
- **Side discipline (the big one).** Long-only means every working order is either a buy to open or a sell to close, and the system never holds a buy and a sell in the same symbol simultaneously (except a genuine stop/target bracket on an *existing* position). Enforce this as an invariant: at most one open order per symbol per side, and never an open buy while there is an open sell in the same symbol unless the sell is a protective exit on a filled position. Spoofing requires orders on the side opposite your real intent; make that impossible.
- **Every order is intended to fill.** Place orders at or inside the spread (at the ask, or at the bid when passive), never "layered" several ticks away in multiple price levels. Do not place more than one resting order per side per symbol. No order sizes beyond what you would be happy to have filled in full.
- **Entering at the ask and repricing is fine.** Buying at the ask is an aggressive order that fills immediately; a marketable limit that then rests because the market ticked away, and is cancel-replaced toward the new ask, is a textbook "good-faith attempt to consummate a trade." Keep the repriced order chasing the market, not retreating from it.
- **Step-down exit ladder (reprice one tick lower every 20 s) is fine in principle** and is the mirror image of spoofing: each replacement moves the sell *toward* the bid, improving the chance of execution, and the order size equals the position you genuinely want to exit. Things that would make it look worse: (i) running it while a buy order in the same name is open; (ii) leaving stale sells far above the market and dropping new ones beneath them (multiple price levels); (iii) extremely rapid cancel-replace (sub-second) that inflates message counts in a thin book; (iv) doing it in extended hours. Keep one working sell, cancel-replace (not cancel-then-new), 20 s cadence is slow by any surveillance standard, and finish with a marketable order if the ladder has not filled within your time budget.
- **Order-to-trade ratio.** Nasdaq's excessive-order fee historically used a weighted order-entry ratio over 100:1 as the trigger; Coscia's 16:1 (1,600%) was damning relative to peers at ~1–2.6:1. Instrument the system to log orders submitted, cancels, replaces and fills per symbol per day; alert if cancels+replaces per fill exceed ~10:1 on any day; investigate your own behavior above ~20:1. A momentum system that chases with a few replacements per entry and a ladder of a few replacements per exit should sit well under 10:1.
- **Thin-book and extended-hours avoidance.** All four 2025–2026 cases exploited thin liquidity (after hours, ADRs, far options series). Trade regular hours only (09:30–16:00 ET), and set a liquidity floor (e.g., no order larger than X% of displayed depth at the touch, and skip symbols below a minimum average daily dollar volume). A $1–$5 universe is already thin; sizing relative to displayed size is the control that keeps your own orders from moving the book.
- **Marking the close.** Do not place or reprice orders in the last ~10 minutes with the aim of ending at a price. Flat-by-close means you must exit before the bell; schedule exits to complete by ~15:45 ET so no sell is working into 15:50–16:00, and never buy after 15:30. Note LULD bands double for Tier 2 stocks under $3 in the last 25 minutes, so prints are noisier then anyway.
- **Self-trades / wash sales.** With one account and the side invariant above, self-matching cannot happen. If a bracket exit and a new entry in the same symbol could ever coexist (e.g., re-entry after a stop), block re-entry while any opposite-side order is open.
- **No cross-broker splitting, no accounts in others' names.** Both were charged as aggravating conduct in Khachatryan and Wang.
- **Respond to broker inquiries truthfully.** Cole's false statements to his firm were pleaded as part of the scheme. Keep the order/decision log so you can explain any trade.
- **Kill switch and message-rate cap** (from FINRA 15-09): a global halt that cancels all open orders, a per-minute order-submission cap, a per-symbol cap, and automatic shutdown on repeated rejects.

**Confidence:** High on the statutory map, the FINRA rule, and the four individual cases (SEC litigation releases retrieved). Medium on the specific numeric thresholds for order-to-trade ratios (the 100:1 Nasdaq fee figure is from 2012 filings and may have changed; there is no published regulatory bright line). High that the step-down ladder and ask-chasing are not spoofing patterns, as a matter of how the cases define the conduct; medium that a given broker's surveillance would never flag them (false positives are possible; the response is documentation, not avoidance).

**Consult a professional?** Not required for the design as scoped. Recommended if you ever add short selling, two-sided/market-making logic, or order placement away from the touch at multiple levels; and immediately if a broker ever sends a trading-pattern inquiry.

## 3. Clearly erroneous executions: when a fill can be unwound

**Short answer.** During regular trading hours (09:30–16:00 ET) a fill in an NMS stock that is subject to the Limit Up-Limit Down plan is **not reviewable** as clearly erroneous when LULD price bands were available and correct at the time, so for your universe an RTH fill is effectively final within seconds. Outside regular hours, or in the rare cases where LULD bands were unavailable, the exchange may break a trade whose price deviates from the reference price by more than the Numerical Guidelines: for stocks priced $0.00–$25.00 the threshold is **10%** in regular hours and **20%** outside regular hours; $25–$50: 5%/10%; >$50: 3%/6%. A review request must reach the exchange within **30 minutes** of execution; exchanges can also act on their own motion, and multi-stock events have wider thresholds. Treat a fill as final unless it printed in extended hours or far from the band.

**Detail.**
- Rule texts: Nasdaq Equity 11 Rule 11890; NYSE Rule 7.10; Cboe BZX/EDGX/EDGA/BYX Rule 11.15; IEX Rule 11.270; FINRA Rules 11892 (exchange-listed) and 11893 (OTC equities). These were harmonized in 2009–2010 (the "$0.00 up to and including $25.00: 10%" table dates from the 2009 harmonization) and amended after LULD: the premise of the current rules is that if LULD bands were in effect and correct, the trade is not eligible for CE review (exceptions: NMS stocks not in LULD such as rights/warrants; transactions outside LULD hours; system-wide outages). Filings in 2026 extend the same LULD-based "trades stand" logic to the new overnight protected bands for 23/5 trading.
- Reference price is normally the consolidated last sale immediately before the transaction(s) in question; exchanges may use a different reference in unusual circumstances.
- Outside regular hours the guidelines double (20% for sub-$25 stocks). Pre-market/after-hours fills in $1–$5 stocks are therefore the ones realistically at risk of being broken.
- Request window: within 30 minutes of execution time, with time, symbol, shares and price. Exchange officials may also nullify on their own initiative, and there are separate provisions for multi-stock events (≥5 stocks: 10%; ≥20 stocks: 30%) and for an exchange-wide "system disruption" review. Appeals go to a CEE panel.
- Broker-level: your counterparty is Alpaca Securities; if an exchange busts a trade, Alpaca reverses the fill in your account (position and cash), typically the same day. I could not retrieve Alpaca's customer agreement language on erroneous trades — **UNCONFIRMED** whether Alpaca reserves additional unilateral rights to cancel executions beyond exchange rulings; read the agreement's "errors" clause.
- FINRA OTC Rule 11893 uses 20% for stocks under $1 and 10% for $5–$75 — not relevant for exchange-listed names but relevant if a holding is delisted to OTC during a hold.

**Design implications.**
- Trade in regular hours only; then CE risk is negligible and fills can be treated as final for position accounting immediately.
- Still, do not mark a position "closed and reconciled" until the broker's end-of-day activity confirms it; keep a reconciliation step at ~16:35 ET and again next morning that compares local fills against Alpaca account activity and flags any cancelled/corrected executions.
- Handle the "fill reversed" event explicitly: if an exit fill is busted after the close you are long overnight in violation of the flat-by-close rule, and if an entry fill is busted you may have an orphaned sell. The system should re-derive positions from the broker, not from its own fill log, before acting.
- Sanity-check your own limit prices against the last trade (e.g., reject any order >5% away from last for sub-$25 stocks). Alpaca's Rule 15c3-5 controls will likely reject such orders anyway, but a local check avoids the reject loop and avoids ever being the counterparty to someone else's erroneous print.

**Confidence:** High on the thresholds and the 30-minute window (confirmed in multiple exchange filings) and on the LULD "not reviewable" premise (2026 NYSE/Cboe filings). Medium on exact current wording in each rulebook (filings may postdate retrieved text). Low on Alpaca-specific terms.

## 4. Beneficial ownership and Section 16: can a small account trip them?

**Short answer.** Yes, realistically, in nano-caps. §13(d)/13(g) trigger at >5% of a class registered under Exchange Act §12 (every listed stock); §16 at >10%. The denominator is **shares outstanding**, not float, but post-reverse-split microcaps now exist with well under 5 million shares outstanding (e.g., Onfolio Holdings at ~850,000 shares after its Aug 2026 1-for-50 split). 5% of 2 million shares at $3 is 100,000 shares = $300,000; at 850,000 shares outstanding, 5% is 42,500 shares ≈ $100k–$200k. A six-figure account trading concentrated positions can cross 5% and even 10% intraday. Crossing triggers filing deadlines measured in business days, and crossing 10% exposes you to **strict-liability** disgorgement of any profit on purchases and sales matched within six months under §16(b) — which an intraday round trip is. There is no intraday or "I sold the same day" exemption.

**Detail.**
- §13(d)/(g) (post-Oct 2023 amendments, Rel. 33-11253): initial Schedule 13D within **5 business days** of crossing 5% (amendments within 2 business days of a material change). A passive investor (no control intent, <20%) may file Schedule 13G under Rule 13d-1(c) within **5 business days** after month-end in which it crossed 5% (13G deadlines effective Sept 30, 2024); 13G amendments 45 days after quarter-end for material changes, and within 2 business days after month-end once ownership exceeds 10% or changes by >5%. Filing cut-off extended to 10 pm ET. Sources differ on whether the passive-investor 10% amendment is 2 or 5 business days — **UNCONFIRMED**, check the adopting release. Keith Gill's June 2024 Schedule 13G for 6.6% of Chewy (9,001,000 shares, Rule 13d-1(c)) is a recent example of an individual retail trader filing.
- Beneficial ownership counts shares you have voting or investment power over, including through options exercisable within 60 days; aggregated across your accounts.
- §16: officers, directors and >10% beneficial owners of a §12-registered class. Form 3 within 10 days of becoming a 10% holder; Form 4 within 2 business days of each subsequent transaction; §16(b) lets the issuer (or any shareholder suing derivatively after a demand, as in Donoghue v. Bulldog) recover profit from any purchase and sale within six months, computed by matching lowest purchase to highest sale, no netting of losses, no knowledge or intent required. The 10% test for §16 purposes requires being >10% both at the purchase and the sale (Foremost-McKesson; Reliance Electric), so a single crossing trade is not itself matched, but every trade while above 10% is. A specialist plaintiffs' bar watches Form 4/13D filings and sends demand letters. Foreign private issuers are exempt from §16 (Rule 3a12-3); many Nasdaq $1–$5 names are FPIs, but do not rely on that without checking the filer status.
- Exchange-listed status guarantees §12 registration, so every symbol in your universe is a §13/§16 class.
- Realism: Nasdaq's continued-listing rules require only 500,000 publicly held shares (Capital Market) and $1 minimum bid; the 2025–2026 wave of 1-for-40 and 1-for-50 reverse splits (Onfolio, Nakamoto, Liminatus, PMGC's repeated splits) means a meaningful tail of listed stocks have 1–5 million shares outstanding. Daily volume in such names is often a few hundred thousand shares, so a position large enough to cross 5% would also be a huge fraction of ADV — a liquidity problem before it is a legal one.

**Design implications.**
- Pull shares outstanding (and ideally float) for every candidate from a data source refreshed at least weekly; store it with the symbol.
- Hard position cap: max shares per symbol ≤ 1% of shares outstanding (gives 5x headroom to the 5% trigger even if the data is stale after a split), and additionally ≤ some percent of 20-day ADV for liquidity. Reject, do not resize up, when shares-outstanding data is missing.
- Detect corporate actions: a reverse split changes shares outstanding by 10–50x overnight; invalidate cached share counts on any split event and re-fetch before trading.
- Aggregate across all accounts you control (including any spouse/joint/IRA) if any of them could hold the same symbol.
- Log maximum intraday ownership percentage per symbol so you can prove you stayed under 5% if ever asked.
- If you ever cross 5% inadvertently, the 13G clock starts; crossing 10% makes the same-day exit a §16(b) matched transaction. Both are reasons the 1% cap should be unconditional.

**Confidence:** High on thresholds, strict liability and the 2023 deadline changes; medium on exact 13G amendment timing for passive investors at 10%; medium on how common sub-2M-share listed stocks are (examples found, no census).

**Consult a professional?** Only if a position ever exceeds 5% of a class (file promptly; the filing itself is simple but the §16 analysis is not).

## 5. SEC/FINRA 2024–2026 AI and automation guidance: does any of it reach an individual?

**Short answer.** No rule or guidance from 2024–2026 regulates an individual's use of AI or automation to trade their own account. The SEC's 2023 "predictive data analytics" conflicts proposal (which targeted broker-dealers and advisers, not investors) was formally withdrawn June 12, 2025. FINRA Regulatory Notice 24-09 (June 2024) and the AI sections of FINRA's 2025 and 2026 Annual Regulatory Oversight Reports address **member firms** only and state that existing, technology-neutral rules apply. The SEC's August 2025 AI Task Force is an internal operations initiative. Enforcement in this space is anti-fraud: "AI-washing" cases against advisers (Delphia, Global Predictions, March 2024) and fraudulent "AI trading bot" schemes against retail victims (Privvy Investments 2026; TSAI Pro 2026). The only way any of this touches you is if you (a) misrepresent AI capabilities to others, or (b) use AI to generate manipulative trading, which Enforcement Director Margaret Ryan named as a 2026 priority. The AI coding agent's role as a developer tool is legally invisible; your obligations as the trader are unchanged.

**Detail.**
- SEC PDA proposal (Rel. 34-97990, Aug 2023): would have required BDs and RIAs to eliminate or neutralize conflicts in "covered technologies" used in investor interactions. Withdrawn June 12, 2025 with 13 other proposals; any future action needs a new proposal.
- FINRA RN 24-09: reminder that Rules 3110 (supervision), 2210 (communications), 4511/17a-4 (records), and 2010 (fair dealing) apply to GenAI use by members; no new requirements. FINRA 2026 Oversight Report (Dec 9, 2025): new standalone GenAI section with expectations for governance, testing, output monitoring, vendor diligence, and first discussion of **AI agents** (risk of agents acting beyond user intent, leaking sensitive data). Again, member-firm scope.
- SEC Aug 1, 2025: AI Task Force under Chief AI Officer Valerie Szczepanik to deploy AI inside the agency; no rulemaking mandate. March 2025 SEC AI roundtable and March 2026 FSOC remarks by Chair Atkins: enforcement against false AI claims continues; no AI-specific rule. Feb 2025: Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit lists AI-enabled fraud first among priorities.
- Joint SEC/NASAA/FINRA investor alert "Artificial Intelligence and Investment Fraud" (Jan 25, 2024): warns investors about unregistered platforms touting AI trading; purely protective.
- Indirect relevance: the Market Access Rule (broker-side) and FINRA 15-09 (member-side) describe the controls regulators expect around automated order flow; brokers increasingly apply equivalent pre-trade controls to API customers.

**Design implications.**
- No AI-specific compliance obligation. Adopt the FINRA 15-09 / 2026 Oversight Report practices voluntarily because they are good engineering: version-controlled strategy code, pre-deployment testing in paper trading, change logs, output logging, model/parameter tracking, a kill switch, and a human-approval gate for strategy changes.
- Keep the AI coding agent out of the production credential path (no API keys in the agent's environment; agent proposes diffs, you review and deploy). This is not a legal requirement; it is how you keep "the agent does not place orders" true in fact.
- Never describe the system publicly in terms of its AI capabilities in connection with any solicitation; that is where AI-washing liability lives.

**Confidence:** High.

**Consult a professional?** No.

## 6. SEC trading suspensions and exchange regulatory halts

**Short answer.** Two different mechanisms. **Exchange halts** (Nasdaq/NYSE) are announced by the listing exchange via the SIP halt message and the exchange's halt page with a reason code: LULD volatility pauses (LUDP) last 5 minutes (extendable 5 more on an imbalance); news-pending (T1) halts last until the news is out and the exchange decides to reopen, often 30–60 minutes but sometimes hours; T12 "additional information requested" halts can last days. **SEC trading suspensions** under Exchange Act §12(k) are announced by an SEC order posted at sec.gov/litigation/suspensions (and on the EDGAR/Trading Suspensions page) with no advance warning, typically effective 9:30 am ET the same or next morning and running the maximum **10 business days** (to 11:59 pm on the end date). During either, your position is frozen: you keep the shares but cannot sell. At Alpaca, open orders are not cancelled during a halt; they are held pending and become eligible to execute when trading resumes, and new orders for a halted name are accepted and queued. After an SEC suspension, an exchange-listed stock resumes on the exchange when the suspension ends (often with a large gap, and the exchange may itself keep the stock halted pending information or move to delist); if the stock has been delisted to OTC, no broker may publish a quote until a Form 211 / Rule 15c2-11 process is completed, which often never happens, leaving holders in the "grey market" with no market maker obligated to trade.

**Detail.**
- LULD: bands are set around a 5-minute reference price; Tier 1 >$3: 5%; Tier 2 >$3: 10%; **$0.75–$3.00: 20%** (both tiers); <$0.75: lesser of $0.15 or 75%. Bands double in the last 25 minutes (15:35–16:00 ET) for Tier 1 and for Tier 2 under $3. A limit state unresolved for 15 seconds triggers a 5-minute pause; the listing exchange may extend if an imbalance persists and reopens via auction. Your $1–$5 universe straddles the $3 boundary: sub-$3 names have 20% bands (doubling to 40% late day), so they move a long way before pausing.
- Nasdaq halt codes (nasdaqtrader.com Trade Halt Codes): T1 News Pending; T2 News Released; T5 Single Stock Trading Pause (10% in 5 minutes, legacy); T6 Extraordinary Market Activity; T8 ETF; T12 Additional Information Requested by Nasdaq; H4/H9/H10/H11 regulatory/SEC-related (H10 = SEC trading suspension); LUDP/LUDS volatility pause; M* market-wide circuit breakers. Dissemination is through the UTP/CTA SIP halt/resume messages and the exchange's current-halts web page; expected resumption time is published when known (T1 typically gives a quote-only period then a reopening time; T12 usually gives none).
- SEC §12(k): up to 10 business days per order; the SEC "cannot announce it is working on a suspension"; grounds include lack of current/accurate information, questions about accuracy of public statements (often promotional campaigns), and suspected manipulation; the Commission need not find the issuer itself violated anything (Efuel EFN, 2019). Orders list the issuer, dates and reason; the SEC's Trading Suspensions page showed its most recent entry dated Oct 14, 2026, so the tool is in active use. Companies frequently file an 8-K the same day.
- After a suspension: exchange-listed securities resume when the suspension expires, but the exchange commonly follows with its own halt (T12) and a delisting determination; for OTC securities, Rule 15c2-11 and FINRA Rule 6432 require a broker-dealer to file Form 211 before quoting, and brokers are prohibited from soliciting purchases in a previously suspended stock until that review is done. Unsolicited sell orders may sometimes be executed on the grey market but there is no obligation on any market maker to fill them. Positions may be marked to zero or to a nominal price by the broker; in a margin account the broker may raise maintenance to 100% on the name.
- Alpaca behavior (from Alpaca's own docs for a halted IPO name and its 24/5 page): "Open orders are held in a pending state during a halt, and they are not automatically canceled"; "Orders submitted for a halted security are accepted and held pending; they execute once the halt is lifted"; the Assets API exposes a halted flag for overnight sessions. **UNCONFIRMED** that identical handling applies to every regulatory halt type on seasoned names and whether cancel requests are honored mid-halt; verify with Alpaca support.

**Design implications.**
- Subscribe to halt/resume status (Alpaca's trading-status stream or a SIP-derived feed) and treat any halt as a state change that (1) cancels all working orders in the symbol so nothing fills blindly on the reopening auction, (2) marks the position as "frozen" with a timestamp and halt code, and (3) blocks new entries until N minutes after resumption. If your exit ladder is working when a T1 or LULD pause hits, a pending limit sell could fill at the reopen far from where you intended; deciding to leave or pull it should be an explicit rule, not an accident.
- Pre-trade screen: skip symbols currently halted, halted earlier that day, or with an SEC suspension or T12 in their recent history; skip names with a reverse split or going-concern/delisting notice in the last 30 days where you can get that data; skip names with an active promotional campaign if you can detect it (SEC suspensions in 2021–2026 cluster around social-media promotions).
- Overnight suspension risk cannot be eliminated for a stock you are holding, but you are flat by close, so the exposure window is intraday: a §12(k) order effective 9:30 am hits only pre-market positions (you have none) and an intraday halt-to-delist is rare. The realistic intraday risk is a T1/T12 halt that persists past 16:00, leaving you long overnight. Plan for it: size positions so one such event is survivable, and have a manual procedure (not automated) for deciding whether to sell on the reopen.
- Margin: if a held name is halted into the close, Alpaca may apply house maintenance changes; keep cash buffer so a frozen position does not force liquidation of others.
- LULD awareness: for sub-$3 names, a 20% band means a momentum exit can gap well beyond your stop before a pause occurs; stop logic should be based on observed prints and limit-state indicators, not on an assumption that a pause will contain the move.

**Confidence:** High on §12(k) mechanics, LULD parameters and Nasdaq halt codes (primary sources). Medium on typical T1/T12 durations (secondary sources). Medium-low on Alpaca's handling of orders during all halt types (one Alpaca FAQ, IPO-specific).

**Consult a professional?** No; operational questions go to Alpaca support.
